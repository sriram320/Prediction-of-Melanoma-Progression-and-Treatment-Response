1. Introduction and Scope
This document is the Week 2 deliverable for our module. Part A reviews the literature on melanoma progression, longitudinal lesion analysis, clinical and dermoscopic imaging, treatment response, temporal AI, 3D morphology and explainable AI, and ends with the research gaps we found. Part B turns those gaps into a project scope, the inputs and outputs of the system, functional and non-functional requirements, and the datasets we can realistically use. It builds on the dataset study from Week 1.
2. Part A - Literature Survey
2.1 Melanoma Progression and Longitudinal Lesion Analysis
In clinical practice, progression is judged by comparing a lesion with its own earlier appearance. Sequential digital dermoscopy photographs selected lesions at intervals, and monitoring over about three months is an accepted way to catch early melanoma: a change usually prompts excision, while stability is reassuring (Yan et al., 2025). Total body photography does the same at body level and helps find new or changing lesions in patients with many naevi.
Learning-based work on sequences is small. Yu et al. (2020) built a spatio-temporal model on serial dermoscopy from 184 patients (747 images, sequences of 1 to 12 images, every lesion confirmed by biopsy), and their 2021 study on 179 lesions found that serial input beat static images and compared favourably with 12 clinicians. Both datasets were self-collected. The PanDerm paper (Yan et al., 2025) tests short-term change detection on SDDI1 (116 lesions, 5 images each, 66 patients) and SDDI2 (229 images in pairs) using a Siamese design, and reports that standardising image quality and alignment mattered: accuracy on SDDI1 was about 0.71 with its pipeline and about 0.60 without. That result supports treating cross-visit registration as a requirement in its own right.
2.2 Clinical and Dermoscopic Imaging
Single-image classification is mature on benchmark data, with recent transformer ensembles reporting accuracies in the high 90s on HAM10000. Kawahara et al. (2019) showed that 7-point checklist features can be predicted together with the diagnosis from paired clinical and dermoscopic images, and later multimodal work such as SkinM2Former extends this with cross-attention. All of these score one time point. One practical point from Chanda et al. (2024) is that HAM10000 contains several images of the same lesion, so lesion-level and patient-level splits are needed to avoid leakage between training and test sets.
2.3 Temporal AI Approaches
Two patterns appear in what we read. The first is pairwise Siamese comparison, used by PanDerm for dermoscopic change detection and by Jin et al. (2021) for pre- and post-treatment MRI, where two subnetworks joined at several layers compare the images. The second is a sequence model over per-visit features, as in Yu et al. Real sequences are short: Yu et al. trained on inputs of 2 to 5 images, and the Basel set has 5. Visit spacing also varies, from about three months in short-term monitoring to roughly 6 to 12 months in long-term programmes, so a model for this project needs to handle short, variable-length sequences and know the time gap between visits.
2.4 3D Morphology and Total Body Photography
3D total body photography (3D-TBP) captures the whole skin surface in one session. AI-supported serial 3D-TBP can flag new or evolving lesions (Gorecki et al., 2025), and a published annotation protocol lists lesion detection, segmentation, matching between sessions, change detection and ugly-duckling detection as target tasks. The main public dataset from this line is SLICE-3D: 401,059 lesion crops (15 mm by 15 mm) from 1,042 patients, only 393 of them malignant, with a patient identifier and TBP-derived measurements such as size and colour. It gives patient context and 3D-derived measurements, but each lesion appears once, so it does not show 3D change over time.
2.5 Treatment Response
Imaging-based response prediction is mostly radiological. In metastatic melanoma, an explainable model combining pre-treatment CT radiomics with serum LDH reached an AUC of 0.89 for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (RECIST at three months), and longitudinal AI-derived PET/CT metrics carried prognostic value under immunotherapy (Sachpekidis et al., 2026). Jin et al. (2021) is a useful template for pairing pre- and post-treatment images. We did not find a public dataset of dermoscopic or clinical skin images before and after treatment with response labels, so for this module treatment response can be designed for but not trained on public data.
2.6 Explainable AI
Grad-CAM-style heatmaps are the standard explanation in dermatology classifiers, sometimes combined with uncertainty estimates, for example EfficientNetV2 with Grad-CAM++ and MC-dropout in MelanomaNet. Chanda et al. (2024) found that dermatologist-like explanations improved clinicians' trust and confidence in melanoma diagnosis. Every explanation method we found is per image; none shows which change between visits drove a prediction.

2.8 Research Gaps
Gap
 | What we found
 | What it means for us
 | 
No public longitudinal dermoscopy at scale
 | HAM10000 and the 2020 ISIC set keep one image per follow-up sequence; the only public serial set found (SDDI1) has 116 lesions; the larger serial sets in the literature are private
 | Training on real sequences starts from very little data; this is the main project risk

Change detection is pairwise and short-term
 | PanDerm-style work compares two images over a short interval; Yu et al. use short sequences
 | Long-horizon progression from variable-length sequences is largely open
 | 
3D data is cross-sectional
 | SLICE-3D has one capture per lesion; we found no public serial 3D set
 | 3D morphology must be derived or kept optional
 | 
Treatment response lacks skin-image data
 | Response models are in CT, MRI and PET; no public pre/post-treatment dermoscopy with labels
 | Design for it, but treat it as conditional
 | 
Explanations are per image
 | Grad-CAM-style methods explain one image, not change between visits
 | A temporal explanation (visit attention plus difference map) needs to be designed
 | 
Skin-tone coverage
 | Reviewed datasets under-represent darker skin types; we have not checked this for the serial sets
 | Subgroup evaluation and a check of metadata are needed, important for Indian skin types

ID
 | Requirement
 | 
NFR-01 (Performance)
 | Risk inference for a single lesion sequence shall complete in a clinically practical time (target: under 60 seconds server-side).
 |

 | The pipeline and model-serving layer shall scale to a growing multi-visit, multi-patient archive without redesign of core components.
 | 
NFR-03 (Reliability/Safety)
 | Any inability to compute a confident prediction shall default to a dermatologist-review flag, never a silent or default-benign output.

Taken together, public data covers the per-visit encoder (HAM10000, ISIC 2020), checklist-feature learning (Derm7pt), patient context and TBP-derived measures (SLICE-3D), and a small amount of real serial data for testing change detection (SDDI1). It does not cover long-horizon progression labels, 3D change over time, or treatment response. Our working plan is to pretrain the encoder on the large static sets with lesion- and patient-level splits, use SDDI1 as the real-sequence benchmark, and treat any synthetic sequences as a development aid only, labelled as such in the results. Sourcing real longitudinal data through a clinical partner is the main open risk.
3.7 Constraints and Feasibility
Each building block (segmentation, registration, CNN or transformer encoders, temporal fusion, Grad-CAM-style explanation, MC-dropout or ensemble uncertainty) has been demonstrated separately in the literature, so the risk is data and integration rather than any single technique. The progression label is also not clean: stable lesions are labelled by follow-up and progressed ones by biopsy. Licensing needs attention too. HAM10000, SIIM-ISIC 2020 and the main SLICE-3D release are CC BY-NC, which suits research but limits commercial use or IP claims; SLICE-3D also has a CC BY "Permissive" subset of 217,477 images. Any non-public data would need ethics approval and, for prospective data, patient consent.
