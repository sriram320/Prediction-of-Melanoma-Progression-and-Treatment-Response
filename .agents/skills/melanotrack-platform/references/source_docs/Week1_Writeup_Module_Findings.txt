1. Module Scope
This module is the groundwork for the project. Before building anything that tracks a lesion over time, we wanted to understand what melanoma looks like clinically and under a dermatoscope, how dermatologists describe it, how change over time is used in follow-up, and what public data exists to train a longitudinal model.
2. Objective
Study melanoma characteristics, dermoscopic terminology and longitudinal lesion monitoring, and identify suitable public datasets that contain multiple images or observations per lesion.
3. Melanoma and Dermoscopy
Melanoma starts in the pigment-producing cells of the skin, and outcomes depend heavily on how early it is removed. The usual clinical screening aid is the ABCDE rule: asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variegation, diameter above roughly 6 mm, and evolution. Four of those five can be judged from one photograph. Evolution cannot, and it is the criterion this project is built around.
Dermoscopy adds magnified, polarised or immersion-lit views of structures below the skin surface, and dermatologists score what they see using pattern analysis, the ABCD rule, the Menzies method or the 7-point checklist (Argenziano et al., 1998). The checklist has three major criteria worth two points each and four minor criteria worth one point each, and a total of three or more is treated as suspicious. These terms matter to us because they are the vocabulary used to label datasets such as Derm7pt, and the same features are what a temporal model should be picking up changes in.

4. Longitudinal Lesion Monitoring
In digital dermoscopic follow-up, patients with many moles have selected lesions photographed at intervals and compared against earlier images. The Vienna programme described with HAM10000 sees patients roughly every 6 to 12 months, and a nevus that showed no change over three visits or 1.5 years was accepted as benign. So stability over time is itself used as evidence, while a lesion that changes is usually excised and confirmed by histopathology. For labels, that means "stable" comes from follow-up and "progressed" from biopsy.

The main finding is that the two biggest public sources deliberately collapse follow-up sequences into a single image: HAM10000 kept the most recent image and the 2020 challenge set kept the median timepoint. The only truly serial public set we found is the small Basel collection, so a model trained on real sequences will start from very little data. Next we will verify access to the Basel images and check the HAM10000 lesion_id groups, and carry these gaps into next week's literature survey.
6. Literature Summary
Static classification is well covered, and Kawahara et al. (2019) showed that dermoscopic checklist features can be predicted alongside the diagnosis using paired clinical and dermoscopic images. Work on sequences is much thinner. Yu et al. (2020) modelled serial dermoscopy on 184 patients and 747 images, with sequence lengths from 1 to 12, and a follow-up study (2021) on 179 histologically confirmed lesions compared the model with 12 dermatologists and reported earlier and more accurate calls than static images. Both used self-collected data that, as far as we could tell, is not public.
